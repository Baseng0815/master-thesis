\section{Successes and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion-interpretation}

The agents manage to learn how to generate structured input and successfully
improve the return over time, showing that the approach of applying deep RL to fuzzing can work.
Nevertheless, the approach also comes with major limitations.

\subsection{Successes}

The results show that the agent is able to learn how to fuzz a PUT.
It is capable of successful credit assignment as illustrated by the input-action targets.
On high-and-low the agent plays high-coverage bytes at a rate above \num{0.99} where a random
policy reaches $\frac{4}{256}\approx\num{0.0156}$~(\cref{sec:results-high-and-low}).
On sequence it recovers the entire 16-byte passcode one position at a time,
with the cost per additional byte growing from three bytes within the first six
iterations to 55 iterations for the last one
alone~(\cref{sec:results-sequence}).

On the corpus-action targets the reward is raw coverage, and the agent manages to yield seeds with
recognizable structure.
The highest-coverage cJSON input is \code{[[[[[2,[[[[[1\ldots}\ and the highest-coverage libxml2
input nests tags to a comparable depth~(\cref{tab:best-inputs}), which the agent derived
itself by optimizing the reward only without any prior baked-in knowledge.
The agent also learned to prefer to generate structural bytes that are useful to the PUT.
For cJSON, the structural byte rate rises from \num{0.08} to a peak of \num{0.90} and
from \num{0.05} to \num{0.75} for libxml2~(\cref{fig:structural-decay}).
A recursive-descent parser rewards nesting with coverage which is what the agent found.

We also showed that planning beats undirected mutation at an identical execution budget.
The cJSON random control reaches a median score of \num{542} covered blocks,
while the baseline reaches \num{645} covered blocks, a clear improvement.
Looking at the fraction of episodes in the baseline run whose best seed reaches the 99th percentile
of the random control run, we see a strong separation by a factor of roughly \num{71}~(\cref{tab:deep-seed}).

\subsection{Limitations}

While the results show that it is in principle feasible to design an RL-based fuzzer,
there are multiple drawbacks to this approach.
The main cost is throughput.
EfficientZero is a very compute-heavy algorithm and requires a careful implementation
to sustain an acceptable execution speed.
But even with an optimal implementation, the cost is still way higher than for
general \gls{cgf} fuzzers like AFL++ due to the many iterations of \gls{mcts}
required to select an action.
In our implementation, the baseline cJSON run executes a total of \num{1664128} inputs in \SI{21.3}{\hour},
and the two 400-iteration libxml2 and picohttpparser campaigns execute a total
of \num{3328128} inputs in \SI{42.1}{\hour} and \SI{40.7}{\hour}, yielding a guest execution rate between
\num{21} and \num{23} which is practically too slow for \gls{cgf}.
A native persistent-mode harness on a similar parser target executes on the order of \num{e4} inputs
per second on one core~\cite{fioraldi_afl_nodate}.
This means that for our approach to be competitive, we must extract roughly
three orders of magnitude more coverage per execution than other \gls{cgf}
fuzzers.
Removing the costly \gls{mcts} search raises the execution rate to about \num{41},
showing that the emulator itself and the learner are the bottlenecks.
We expect it to be possible to improve the throughput of our implementation by
an order of magnitude since execution resources are not fully utilized, but
this remains an engineering challenge and part of future work.

Despite planning being an integral part of EfficientZero, we found planning to not
contribute much to the targets tested here.
For the cJSON ablation, coverage is flat across simulation counts: \num{687}, \num{640} and \num{645} blocks at 50, 100
and 200 simulations.
The rapidly falling KL divergence between the MCTS root visit count
distribution and policy prior shown in~\cref{fig:policy-improvement} provides a
quantification of this problem.
The falling KL in this case shows planning being less and less useful as time goes on.
It is important to mention though that planning has a major effect in the first few iterations.
We hypothesize that planning with a high number of \gls{mcts} iterations only
becomes useful for campaigns on larger PUTs with more complicated behavior and
more training budget to learn this behavior.

For picohttpparser, coverage does not grow much with input structure.
This makes it basically impossible to learn well-formed inputs.
picohttpparser ends at around \num{81} covered blocks for all four seeds,
reaches \SI{90}{\percent} of that ceiling within \SI{25}{\percent} of its
budget and gains nothing in the final
quarter~(\cref{fig:saturation,tab:campaign-inventory}).
This is a property of the PUT itself, showing the success of a fuzzing campaign
and the usefulness of optimizing for the given reward to be highly target-dependent.
The same holds for other \gls{cgf} fuzzers, but they can better overcome this problem
due to their high execution rate and therefore higher likelihood of finding interesting seeds
with novel coverage.

Another problem lies in a progressive decay of performance for the agent.
The structural byte rate peaks before the end of training on all three corpus targets,
but finishes below this peak in the end~(\cref{fig:structural-decay}).
During experimentation, we found learning to sometimes converge onto a bad policy
with entropy collapsing to zero.
One failure mode we often encountered was the agent producing a slight majority
of action $a_i$, which then found its way into the replay buffer, and under uniform
sampling, $a_i$ then is overtrained and all other actions $a_{j\neq i}$ are undertrained,
further nudging the policy towards $a_i$, causing a policy collapse\index{policy collapse} onto $a_i$ in the end.
This is mostly solved by prioritized experience replay.
In our case, it is not just an improvement to learning speed, but prevents the
agent policy from collapsing by promoting training on actions that are underrepresented
in the replay buffer.
Increasing the temperature and Dirichlet noise ratio also helps in this regard.

Another limitation is presented through the encoding of actions and observations.
While we can model the selection of a mutation location and value by a two-step process
in the reduced single-seed setting, it is not easy to scale this to a whole seed corpus.
It is also not clear how to include the seed corpus state for multiple seeds in the
observations to allow the agent to learn useful actions.
Consider a corpus of 32 seeds, each of length 16.
A single action then has to decide whether to retain the previous test case, which
new seed to select, and what mutations to apply.
In this example, the resulting (non-hierarchical) action space has a size of about

\[
  \lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert=2\cdot 32\cdot 16\cdot 256=262144,
\]

which is way too large for a vanilla EfficientZero agent.
Approaches to solve this problem are outlined in~\cref{chap:future-work}.
